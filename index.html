Model assumptions
  • Steady flight: constant speed, constant flight path angle and constant turn rate (no acceleration along the path).
  • Thrust acts along the flight path. Coordinated turns without sideslip.
  • Angle of attack is an output: it is the angle that gives the lift needed for the chosen weight, speed, climb and turn.
  • ISA atmosphere (optionally offset by ΔT), no wind.
  • Parabolic drag polar plus wave drag; aerodynamic coefficients are estimates tuned to published performance, not manufacturer data.
  • Simple thrust lapse with density and Mach number; no engine deck. For the Cessna, engine power with a fixed propeller efficiency. Trim drag, store drag, windmilling-propeller drag and Reynolds-number effects are not modelled.
  • Lift and drag curves are only modelled up to the stall.

User guide

What this tool shows

It calculates the forces on an aircraft in steady flight: flying straight or in a turn, climbing or descending at a constant speed. You choose the aircraft, how heavy it is, where and how fast it flies and what path it follows. The tool works out how much lift the wing must make, the angle of attack needed for that lift, the drag that results and the thrust the engines must deliver.

Why angle of attack is a result, not an input

Once weight, speed, altitude, climb and turn are fixed, the lift is fixed too (it must balance weight and supply the turning force). For a given wing there is only one angle of attack that produces that lift at that speed. Setting the angle of attack as well would over-constrain the problem, so the tool reports it instead. In a real aircraft the pilot sets the angle of attack with the elevator and the speed then follows; here we fix the speed and let the angle of attack follow. Both describe the same equilibrium.

How to use it

  • Pick an aircraft and a scenario to load typical values, then drag any slider or type a number. Everything updates immediately.
  • Speed can be entered in m/s, km/h, knots or Mach number. It is the true airspeed. Equivalent and calibrated airspeed are shown in the hint and the table.
  • Climb can be given as a flight path angle γ or as a climb rate. Negative values mean descent. The Glide button finds the descent angle at which no thrust is needed: the engine-out (or sailplane) glide.
  • Turn can be given as bank angle, load factor or turn radius; the other two follow. Bank 0 means straight flight.
  • Red messages mean that no steady flight exists (for example the wing would stall). Amber messages flag limits that are exceeded but where the physics still works, such as too much weight or not enough thrust.
  • Click a section title to fold it away; folded sections are not recalculated. Expand all / Collapse all and Layout are at the top. The « button hides the input panel to give the charts more room; the strip on the left brings it back. The page remembers these choices in this browser.

Reading the views

  • Side view: the dashed horizontal line is the horizon, the long dashed line the flight path. γ is the angle between them; α is the angle between the flight path and the aircraft's body axis. Pitch θ ≈ γ + α. Thrust and drag are usually much smaller than lift and weight, so they are magnified by the factor written in the corner.
  • Rear view: the aircraft banks by φ. Lift splits into a vertical part, L cos φ, that holds the aircraft up, and a horizontal part, L sin φ, that pulls it round the turn. The load factor n = L/W grows quickly with bank: 60° bank needs 2 g.

Reading the charts

  • CL–α and CD–α: how lift and drag grow with angle of attack for each configuration, up to the stall. Flaps shift the lift curve up (more lift at the same α) and raise the maximum lift, at the cost of more drag. Beyond the stall real wings behave very differently (a sudden lift drop on thin airliner sections, a gentle one on thick sections, little drop on a delta with vortex lift), so the curves stop there.
  • Range parameter: for jets, M·L/D at several Mach numbers; its peak gives the best range (Breguet). For the propeller aircraft and the sailplane, L/D (best range or best glide) and CL1.5/CD (best endurance or minimum sink).
  • Thrust available vs Mach: how engine thrust changes with speed and altitude: falling with speed for turbofans and propellers, rising for an afterburning fighter engine. Dotted lines show the thrust needed for level flight; where they meet, the aircraft reaches its top speed at that altitude.
  • Flight envelope (altitude vs Mach): where level flight is possible, bounded by the stall, the speed limits and the thrust, up to the ceiling. The contours show specific excess power Ps: the climb rate available at each point. Hover over the chart to read values.
  • Drag polars: lift coefficient against drag coefficient. The line from the origin that just touches a polar marks the best lift-to-drag ratio. Polars at higher Mach numbers move right because of wave drag.
  • Drag vs Mach: splits drag into parasite, wave and induced parts as speed changes at your weight and altitude.
  • Thrust/power required vs speed: the classic "drag bucket". The minimum of the thrust curve is the minimum-drag speed (best glide, best endurance for jets); the minimum of the power curve is the minimum-power speed (minimum sink for a sailplane). Where required thrust meets available thrust is the maximum (or minimum) speed.
  • V–n diagram: the safe combinations of speed and load factor.
  • Turn performance: how fast the aircraft can turn at each speed, limited by stall, structure and thrust. The corner where the stall and structural limits meet is the corner speed: the fastest instantaneous turn.

What the results mean

Load factor nLift divided by weight. 1 in straight level flight, cos γ / cos φ in a steady climbing turn.
CL, CDLift and drag coefficients: lift or drag divided by dynamic pressure q and wing area S.
CD0, CD,wave, CD,iParasite drag (friction and form, including flaps and gear), compressibility drag, and lift-induced drag.
L/DLift-to-drag ratio. Equal to the glide ratio with engines off.
EAS, CASEquivalent airspeed (same dynamic pressure at sea-level density) and calibrated airspeed (what a pitot-static instrument shows).
Specific excess power Ps(Available − required thrust) × V / W. The climb rate the aircraft could add at this speed, in m/s.
SEP contoursLines of equal specific excess power on the altitude–Mach chart. The Ps = 0 line is the thrust limit; the highest values sit at low altitude and moderate Mach number. For a sailplane the contours show sink rate instead.
Stall speedSpeed at which the required CL equals CLmax. It grows with √n, so it is higher in a turn.
VMO / MMO, VFE, VNEMaximum operating speed / Mach number, maximum flap (or gear) speed, never-exceed speed.

How the numbers are calculated

Tap a step to open it. The steps run in the same order as the program.

1. Atmosphere
(1.1)TISA = 288.15 − 0.0065·h, p = 101 325·(TISA/288.15)5.25588 (up to 11 km)
(1.2)11–20 km: TISA = 216.65 K, p = 22 632·exp[−g(h − 11 000)/(RTISA)]
(1.3)T = TISA + ΔT, ρ = p/(RT), a = √(γRT), σ = ρ/ρ0
(1.4)Viscosity (Sutherland): μ = 1.458·10−6·T1.5/(T + 110.4)

R = 287.05 J/(kg·K), γ = 1.4, g = 9.80665 m/s², ρ0 = 1.225 kg/m³. A temperature deviation keeps the pressure of the standard atmosphere and changes the density.

2. Mass and weight
(2.1)m = mOE + mpayload + xfuel·mfuel,max, W = mg

xfuel is the fuel on board as a fraction of full tanks. For the sailplane it is the water ballast. The table also shows the fuel weight fraction Wfuel/W.

3. Speeds
(3.1)M = V/a, q = ½ρV² = ½γpM²
(3.2)EAS: VE = V√σ
(3.3)Impact pressure: qc = p[(1 + 0.2M²)3.5 − 1] (M ≤ 1; Rayleigh pitot formula above)
(3.4)CAS: the speed that gives the same qc at sea-level p0, a0 (solved numerically)
(3.5)Reynolds number: Re = ρVc̄/μ, c̄ = S/b
4. Flight path, turn and load factor

Forces in the vertical plane through the flight path and in the horizontal plane, for a steady climbing turn without sideslip:

(4.1)Along the path: T = D + W sin γ
(4.2)Normal, vertical plane: L cos φ = W cos γ
(4.3)Horizontal (centripetal): L sin φ = m(V cos γ)²/R
(4.4)n = L/W = cos γ / cos φ, tan φ = V² cos γ / (gR), ω = V cos γ / R
(4.5)Climb rate: vc = V sin γ

Any one of bank angle, load factor or radius fixes the other two. A load factor below cos γ cannot give a steady turn.

5. Lift and angle of attack
(5.1)CL = nW/(qS)
(5.2)Subsonic lift slope (DATCOM/Helmbold): CLα = kb·2πA / {2 + √[4 + (Aβ/η)²(1 + tan²Λ½/β²)]}, β = √(1 − M²)
(5.3)Supersonic (fighter): CLα = kb·(4/β)(1 − 1/(2Aβ)), β = √(M² − 1), blended over M 0.95–1.15
(5.4)Linear part: CL = CLα(α − α0) up to r·CL,max
(5.5)Rounded top: CL = CL,max − k(αs − α)², αs = α0 + (2 − r)CL,max/CLα
(5.6)CL,max(M) = CL,max,config − [CL,max,clean(0) − CL,max,clean(M)]

kb accounts for body and canard lift, A is the aspect ratio, η = 0.95. α0 for each configuration is chosen so that the curve stalls at the configuration's stall angle αs at low speed; flaps make α0 more negative. The clean CL,max falls with Mach number (buffet onset). The angle of attack is found by inverting (5.4)/(5.5). If the needed CL exceeds CL,max, no steady flight exists.

6. Drag
(6.1)CD = CD0 + ΔCD0,config + CD,wave + KCL², D = qSCD
(6.2)Subsonic: K = 1/(πAe); supersonic: K = 1/CLα (no leading-edge suction), blended over M 0.9–1.1
(6.3)Korn: MDD = κ/cos Λ − (t/c)/cos²Λ − CL/(10 cos³Λ)
(6.4)Lock: Mcr = MDD − (0.1/80)1/3, CD,wave = 20(M − Mcr)⁴ for M > Mcr
(6.5)Fighter: CD,wave rises as sin² from M 0.86 to a peak of 0.024 at M 1.15, then falls slowly

κ is the airfoil technology factor (0.95 for supercritical sections), Λ the quarter-chord sweep, t/c the thickness ratio. The wave drag depends on CL, so it grows when the wing works harder.

7. Thrust and fuel flow
(7.1)High-bypass turbofan: Tav = TSLS·σ0.85(1 − 0.4M + 0.1M²)
(7.2)Fighter dry: Tav = TSLS,dry·σ0.8(1 + 0.25M²); reheat: TSLS,wet·σ0.8(1 + 0.3M²)
(7.3)Piston + propeller: P = PSL(1.132σ − 0.132), Tav = [Ts−8 + (ηP/V)−8]−1/8, ṁf = BSFC·TV/η
(7.4)Fuel flow: ṁf = c·T, c = TSFC (rises with Mach number; reheat blended between dry and full reheat)

These lapse rates are rough fits that give realistic climb and cruise thrust. Real engines have detailed performance decks. For the propeller, available thrust is the static thrust Ts at low speed and ηP/V at higher speed, blended smoothly; the power falls with altitude by the Gagg–Ferrar rule.

8. Performance values
(8.1)Preq = TreqV, Ps = (Tav − Treq)V/W
(8.2)Max climb angle at this speed: sin γmax ≈ (Tav − D)/W
(8.3)Glide with zero thrust: sin γ = −D/W (iterated, since D depends on n = cos γ/cos φ)
(8.4)Stall speed: Vs = √[2nW/(ρSCL,max(M))] (iterated in M)
(8.5)Breguet range: R = [V/(gc)]·(L/D)·ln[W/(W − Wfuel)], endurance t = mfuel/ṁf
(8.6)Jet range parameter: R = [a/(gc)]·M(L/D)·ln(W0/W1), altitude for a chosen CL: p = W/(0.7SM²CL)
(8.7)Propeller range: R = [η/(g·BSFC)]·(L/D)·ln(W0/W1); sink rate ∝ √(W/S)·CD/CL1.5

Minimum-drag and minimum-power speeds and (L/D)max are found by searching over speed or CL, so they include wave drag. Range and endurance assume the whole fuel load is flown at today's L/D and speed.

9. Charts

Each chart repeats steps 1 to 8 while varying one quantity, with all other inputs at your settings. CL–α, CD–α and the polars use the current Mach number. The thrust chart keeps your climb and turn input (so a fixed climb rate or bank is kept at every speed). The V–n and turn charts are for level flight at your altitude and weight. The flight envelope repeats the level-flight calculation at 81 altitudes: stall Mach from (8.4) with n = 1 and 1.3, the speed limit from VMO (as calibrated airspeed, converted with (3.4)) and MMO, and the Mach range where available thrust ≥ drag. The SEP contours evaluate (8.1) on a 91 × 71 grid of Mach number and altitude inside the envelope, and lines of equal Ps are traced through the grid cells (marching squares). The thrust chart evaluates (7.1)–(7.3) at six fixed altitudes.
